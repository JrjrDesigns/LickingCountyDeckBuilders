Deck Permit Guide
Licking County, Ohio

What you need to know about permits before building a deck in Newark, Pataskala, Granville, Heath, Hebron, Johnstown, or anywhere in Licking County.

When Do You Need a Deck Permit in Licking County?

Most residential decks in Licking County require a building permit. The general rule under Ohio Residential Code: a permit is required for any deck that is attached to the house, elevated more than 30 inches above finished grade at any point, or larger than 200 square feet.

In practice, this covers the overwhelming majority of decks built on Licking County homes. A ground-level floating deck, unattached and under 200 sq ft, might not require a permit — but as soon as you attach it to the house or exceed that size threshold, you're in permit territory.

In our experience working throughout Licking County, the homeowners who skip permits usually do so based on a neighbor's advice or a contractor who told them they could get away with it. It works — until they try to sell the home, file an insurance claim after a collapse, or get a complaint from a neighbor.

Which Building Department Handles Your Permit?

Licking County is not a single jurisdiction for building permits. Depending on where your property is located, your permit comes from:

  • City of Newark Building Department — all properties within Newark city limits (43055, 43056, 43058)
  • City of Heath Building Department — all properties within Heath city limits (43056)
  • Village of Granville Zoning Office — Granville village properties (43023); historic district properties may face additional review
  • Village of Pataskala Building Department — properties within Pataskala village limits (43062)
  • Village of Johnstown Building Department — properties within Johnstown village limits (43031)
  • Licking County Building Department — all unincorporated areas, townships, and communities without their own building department (including most of Hebron)

We pull permits through all of these offices regularly and know what each one requires in terms of drawings, footing specs, and inspection scheduling.

What the Permit Process Looks Like

For a typical residential deck in Licking County, the permit process involves submitting a site plan, a structural drawing showing joist size and spacing, ledger connection detail, and footing specifications. Some offices require a plot plan showing setbacks from property lines.

Once submitted, review typically takes 5–14 business days depending on the jurisdiction and current workload. Permits are then issued and inspections are scheduled at key stages:

  • Footing inspection — after holes are dug, before concrete is poured. Inspector verifies depth meets frost line requirements (36–42 inches in Licking County).
  • Framing inspection — after structural framing is complete but before decking is installed. Inspector checks joist sizing, ledger connection, hardware, and post bases.
  • Final inspection — after all work including railing is complete. Inspector confirms code compliance across the full structure.

We schedule and are present for all inspections. You don't need to take time off work to meet the inspector — we handle it.

Setback Requirements in Licking County

Decks must comply with property setback requirements, which vary by jurisdiction and zoning district. In most Licking County residential zones, rear yard setbacks for structures are 10–25 feet from the property line. Side yard setbacks vary.

Before designing your deck layout, we check your property's setback requirements as part of the free estimate visit. This is one of the most common surprises we see — homeowners who planned a certain size deck and then discover their lot doesn't accommodate it at the required setback distance. Getting this right at the design stage saves significant cost and frustration.

What Happens If You Build Without a Permit?

Building a deck without a required permit in Licking County creates several real problems:

  • Stop-work orders — If the county or city becomes aware of unpermitted construction, they can order work to stop and require retroactive permits or demolition.
  • Fines — Municipalities can levy fines for unpermitted work, sometimes based on the value of the structure.
  • Home sale complications — When you sell your home, unpermitted structures must be disclosed. Buyers' lenders often require the structure to be permitted, demolished, or a portion of the sale price escrowed until compliance is achieved.
  • Insurance issues — If someone is injured on an unpermitted deck, your homeowner's insurance may dispute the claim based on code non-compliance.
  • Structural risk — The permit process exists to catch structural deficiencies. A deck that bypasses inspection may have undersized joists, inadequate footings, or improper ledger attachment — the leading cause of deck collapses.

Why You Should Let Your Contractor Pull the Permit

When a licensed contractor pulls the permit, they take responsibility for the work meeting code. This is significant: if an inspector flags a problem, it's on the contractor to fix it at no additional cost to you.

Some homeowners pull their own permits to save money. This transfers responsibility to the homeowner. If inspections reveal deficiencies in the contractor's work, you're negotiating against a contractor who may not agree with the inspector's findings — and you're the permit holder, not them.

Licking County Deck Builders pulls every permit on every project we do. We submit the drawings, pay the permit fees, schedule inspections, and are on site when the inspector arrives. This is included in our project cost — not an add-on fee.
