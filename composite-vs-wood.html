Composite vs. Wood Decking:
An Honest Comparison

Written for Licking County homeowners making the choice between composite and pressure-treated wood — with real cost numbers, real maintenance expectations, and honest tradeoffs.

The Honest Overview

We build both composite and wood decks in Licking County, and we tell homeowners the same thing every time: there is no universally "better" material. The right choice depends on your budget, how long you plan to be in the home, how much time you want to spend on maintenance, and what the deck will look like in the context of your home.

What we try to avoid is oversimplifying — telling every homeowner "composite is always worth it" or "wood is fine if you maintain it." Both are true in specific circumstances. Here's the full picture.

Cost Comparison

This is usually where the conversation starts. For a typical 300–400 sq ft deck in Licking County:

MaterialInstalled Cost (300–400 sq ft)Annual MaintenanceExpected Lifespan
Pressure-Treated Wood$8,500–$14,000$200–$600 (stain/seal every 2–3 yrs)15–25 years
Trex Select (entry composite)$13,000–$18,000Minimal (soap and water)25+ years

When customers see those numbers side by side, the first reaction is often "wood is clearly cheaper." But we ask them to run the math on 20 years of staining, one deck board replacement project, and potentially a full re-deck of the surface in year 15 — and then compare that to a composite with a 25-year warranty and zero maintenance. The gap narrows significantly for long-term owners.

The Maintenance Question

This is where composite wins decisively for the right homeowner. A properly capped composite deck — Trex Transcend, TimberTech Azek, Fiberon Paramount — requires nothing beyond occasional soap-and-water cleaning. No staining, no sealing, no sanding, no board replacement from rot.

Pressure-treated wood in Ohio's climate requires real maintenance. Here's what "maintained" actually means:

  • Allow new pressure-treated wood to dry for 6–12 months before first stain application
  • Clean and re-apply deck stain or sealer every 2–3 years
  • Inspect and treat for any cracking, checking, or soft spots annually
  • Replace individual boards as they deteriorate — common in year 12–18 on a wood deck

Many Licking County homeowners intend to maintain their wood decks and then don't — life gets busy, the staining gets deferred year after year. This is actually the most common scenario we see when we're called to replace a wood deck. The structure is fine but the surface has deteriorated badly from years of no treatment in Ohio's freeze-thaw cycle.

Performance in Ohio's Climate

Ohio is a challenging environment for outdoor building materials. The freeze-thaw cycle — water saturates the wood, freezes, expands, and repeats — is harder on exterior materials than sustained cold or sustained heat.

Composite decking handles this much better than wood. The material doesn't absorb water, so there's nothing to freeze and expand. The structural framing under a composite deck is still pressure-treated wood (as it is under any deck), but the surface you walk on won't check, split, or warp from moisture cycling.

Wood decking can handle Ohio's climate well — when properly built, properly dried before first treatment, and consistently maintained. It's the "consistently maintained" part that catches most homeowners.

Appearance

This used to be a category where wood won clearly. Early composite decking looked artificial and faded badly. Modern capped composite is a different product — the grain patterns, color variation, and texture of premium composite products like Trex Transcend or TimberTech Vintage are genuinely attractive.

That said, natural wood has a warmth and character that composite still doesn't fully replicate. If you have a craftsman-style home, a historic property (common in Granville), or a design aesthetic that calls for real wood, we understand that preference and build beautiful wood decks.

The color-fade question is worth addressing: older composite faded badly. Capped composite products from reputable brands include a color-stable cap layer that holds color well over 25 years. We see faded composite on homes that were built with entry-level, uncapped products from 15–20 years ago — those products are largely no longer on the market.

Which Should You Choose?

After building decks across Licking County, here's our honest recommendation matrix:

  • Choose composite if you're staying in the home 10+ years, you don't want to think about deck maintenance, you want a 25-year warranty, or your budget can absorb the upfront premium.
  • Choose wood if your budget is the primary constraint, you genuinely enjoy seasonal home maintenance, you're building on a short timeline before selling, or the aesthetic of your home calls for natural materials.
  • Either works for most Licking County homes — the framing structure is identical. A well-built wood deck is a good deck. A well-built composite deck is a better deck for most long-term homeowners.

We give every homeowner an estimate on both materials. The decision is yours, and we'll build whichever you choose with the same level of care.
